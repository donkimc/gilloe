# Gilloe Complete Project Guide

**Part I — Strategy, Business Model, Economics, and Marketing**

## A Korea-First Real-World Adventure Platform: Strategy, Economics, and Marketing

**Published:** September 10, 2026  
**Stage:** Concept validation  
**Scope:** Product strategy, business model, market assessment, and launch communication

> Walk into the story.

Gilloe explores a simple possibility: an ordinary afternoon in a familiar neighborhood could become a shared adventure. A café receipt becomes evidence. A detail on a building becomes a clue. A conversation with a friend changes the interpretation of a mystery.

Gilloe’s ambition is a platform where local creators design these experiences, players discover and purchase them, and participating businesses become meaningful parts of the story.

Gilloe is at the proposal stage; this document does not announce a launched service. Game examples, prices, budgets, revenue shares, and performance targets below are planning assumptions. Competitor descriptions are supported by public sources reviewed on September 10, 2026. Korean copy is included intentionally in the marketing sections; the main document is in English.

## TL;DR

- **The concept:** Smartphone-guided adventures combining real places, stories, puzzles, and cooperation.
- **The first audience:** Korean residents in their twenties and thirties looking for something new to do as a couple or a group of friends.
- **The first experiment:** One 60–90 minute Seongsu mystery, approximately 1.5–2 km long, with 5–7 stops for teams of 2–4.
- **The business vision:** Connect players, creators, and local businesses through paid games, creator tools, and carefully integrated sponsorships.
- **The competitive reality:** This is an existing category. International creator platforms and Korean AI-assisted experience platforms already operate. The opportunity must be earned through experience quality and local execution.
- **The decision:** GO for a small validation experiment. The first commercial milestone is **20 genuinely paying teams outside the founder’s personal network**. That is an early signal, not proof of product-market fit.
- **The economics:** Illustrative scenarios connect 3,000 / 30,000 / 300,000 active users with host and store economics; the middle case produces about ₩1.45 million monthly operating profit under explicit assumptions.
- **The infrastructure:** Start with a Seoul backend; add global content delivery and regional game operations as actual demand requires.
- **The player promise:** A satisfying shared experience, with essential clues included in the ticket price and no compulsory store purchase to finish.

## 1. Executive Summary

Gilloe is a proposed platform that turns neighborhoods into playable environments. Players follow a story through real locations, discover evidence, solve puzzles, and reach a final objective. Games could include mysteries, treasure hunts, romance, history, fantasy, or cooperative social experiences.

The immediate product is one entertaining outing. The longer-term business is a three-sided marketplace:

| Participant | Value received | Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Players | Memorable activities with clear expectations and reliable execution | Purchases, feedback, referrals, and repeat participation |
| Creators | Tools, distribution, and a path to earnings | Original stories, local knowledge, puzzles, and maintenance |
| Local businesses | Relevant visits and optional commercial opportunities | Approved locations, contextual experiences, and rewards |

Gilloe’s initial strategy focuses on Korean locals rather than positioning the service primarily as a tourism application. The purchase occasion is practical: “What should we do together this weekend?”

Seongsu is a proposed first test area because its cafés, creative retail, and walking experiences offer useful narrative material. Seoul’s official tourism coverage supports that description, although it does not establish demand for this particular product. [Visit Seoul: Exploring Seongsu](https://english.visitseoul.net/editorspicks/2026-Seongsu/ENNky3epf)

The category is competitive. Questo already combines walking stories with creator participation. Realworld Studio in Korea advertises AI-assisted creation and location-related interactive tools. These findings materially narrow any claim that the combination itself is novel. [Questo creators](https://questoapp.com/creators), [Realworld Studio](https://studio.realworld.to/)

The right next investment is therefore a small, measurable experiment. A good first game can test enjoyment and payment. A subsequent merchant pilot can test operational fit. Creator tools and a broad marketplace should follow evidence that those foundations work.

## 2. Concept and Player Experience

### The core loop

**Choose an adventure → receive the briefing → visit a location → notice something → solve a puzzle → discover new evidence → discuss with teammates → reach a conclusion.**

The physical setting should matter to the solution. If a game can be completed just as effectively from a sofa, the location has probably become decoration rather than gameplay.

The phone provides instructions, evidence, hints, and progress. The most important activity happens between people and in the surrounding place. Short passages, optional audio with transcripts, and a simple evidence notebook can help preserve that balance.

### What a game listing must explain

Every listing should show duration, walking distance, number of stops, difficulty, supported language, team size, starting point, and playable hours. It should also explain stairs or access limitations, age suitability, weather restrictions, and any optional spending.

Prices must state whether they apply per person or per team. Scheduled events need an arrival time, minimum group size, cancellation terms, and a plan for absent participants. Self-guided experiences should state any operating-hour restrictions rather than promising unrestricted availability.

### Two formats

**Self-guided adventures** allow a private group to start within a published operating window. They are the simplest format for the pilot.

**Scheduled social adventures** bring participants together at a set time. Different roles can receive different evidence, creating a reason to communicate. They offer richer social possibilities but require more coordination and support.

## 3. The Problem

Gilloe begins with four hypotheses about unmet needs. These require interviews and observed behavior, rather than assumptions about the entire Korean market.

| Group | Proposed problem | Product response to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumers | Planning another date or group outing feels repetitive | An easy-to-understand, story-led activity in a familiar area |
| Creators | Turning a story into a functioning outdoor experience requires several different skills | Templates, editing support, testing tools, and distribution |
| Small businesses | Advertising attention does not necessarily become a useful visit | A contextual visit with measurable, optional purchasing |
| Cultural and tourism organizations | Visitors may pass through without engaging deeply | Missions that encourage observation and exploration |

The alternatives include escape rooms, movies, board-game cafés, pop-ups, walking tours, and simply visiting a café. Winning a consumer’s afternoon requires being appealing against these substitutes, even when they use very different technology.

## 4. Target Users

### First priority: local couples and friends

Recruit adults in their twenties and thirties who already enjoy puzzles, mysteries, or trying new activities. Start with teams of 2–4 so the outing feels easy to organize.

Useful interview questions concern their last actual outing: how they chose it, what they paid, who organized it, what caused frustration, and when they next expect to book an activity. General enthusiasm for an idea is weaker evidence than a recent purchase or a real booking.

### Secondary audiences

| Audience | Potential fit | When to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Escape-room and mystery enthusiasts | Higher interest in clue quality and deduction | During early content testing |
| International residents and visitors | English-language social exploration | After the Korean route works reliably |
| University clubs and language exchanges | Structured cooperation | After private-group testing |
| Employers | Team-building and facilitated events | As a separate paid pilot |
| Families | Daytime discovery with age-appropriate stories | With a dedicated family design |
| Tourism bodies and festivals | Sponsored place-based engagement | Once an operating case study exists |

Each audience has different requirements. A casual date, a children’s hunt, and a corporate event should not share one undifferentiated product brief.

## 5. Korea Market Assessment

### Why a Korean pilot is plausible

Seoul offers neighborhoods where a short route can connect contrasting public spaces and commercial settings. Seongsu is particularly relevant to this proposal: official tourism material describes its industrial heritage, cafés, concept stores, and temporary retail experiences. That is evidence of suitable settings, not a count of addressable customers. [Visit Seoul](https://english.visitseoul.net/editorspicks/2026-Seongsu/ENNky3epf)

Local precedents also show that outdoor mysteries and mission-based experiences are established formats:

- **QuesTour** lists outdoor escape games and QR/GPS stamp tours. Its portfolio includes *북촌에 귀신이 산다*, a Bukchon mystery experience. [QuesTour](https://questour.kr/index), [Portfolio](https://questour.net/portfolio)
- **Saisiot** describes mobile outdoor play and a kit supporting up to four participants in its 2023 FAQ. This is a historical product reference; current availability should be checked before booking. [Saisiot FAQ](https://saisiot.com/home/noti/m3_detail/80/)
- **Our Neighborhood Treasure Hunt** describes AR missions, QR-based rewards, and small-business promotion in its developer-provided app listing. [Google Play listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=kr.smartorder.bomul)
- **TourPlay** was profiled by the Seoul Tourism Organization in 2020 as a platform for user-designed location missions and rewards. This establishes a precedent, not its current scale or operating status. [Visit Seoul: ELFE / TourPlay](https://english.visitseoul.net/partners-en/startup-project-2020/ELFE/ENN036584)
- **Gyeonggi Treasure Hunting** was reported as a 2025 museum pilot using QR, AR, and NFC. Realworld also lists the museum project in its portfolio. [2025 reporting](https://v.daum.net/v/20250810150638340), [Realworld project](https://business.realworld.to/ja/projects/31c18b2e-ed60-80cf-aae7-e05953f275e0)
- **Gyeonggi Culture Pass** added QR visit missions with rewards in its April 2026 update. This is additional evidence of public-sector experimentation with place-based participation. [Gyeonggi official announcement](https://gnews.gg.go.kr/news/news_detail.do?SchMonth=&SchYear=&number=202604161614017401C049&page=1&s_code=C049)

These examples demonstrate activity in the category. They do not prove that consumers will pay sustainable prices for a new independent platform. Free, subsidized, and sponsored participation should be analyzed separately from paid consumer demand.

### Geographic focus

Start with one walkable part of Seongsu. Hongdae/Yeonnam, Ikseon-dong, Bukchon, Euljiro, Gangnam, Busan, and Jeju remain candidate expansion areas from the concept discussion, not committed launches.

Choose a route based on safe pedestrian access, durable clues, quiet places to stop, dependable opening hours, and proximity to transport. Fashionable foot traffic alone is insufficient. Residential areas require particular attention to noise and disruption.

### Market sizing without false precision

No defensible total market estimate has been established for this exact offering. A useful first model is operational:

**Monthly ticket revenue = active routes × paid team starts per route per month × average realized team price.**

For illustration, three routes selling 100 team starts each at ₩7,900 would produce **₩2,370,000 in gross monthly ticket sales**. These are assumed inputs, not a forecast. Marketplace revenue would be only the retained share, and profit would be lower after costs.

## 6. Blue-Ocean Analysis

The broad idea is **not a blue ocean**. Walking games, outdoor escape rooms, location missions, and creator tools already exist. Even AI-assisted creation cannot safely be described as an uncontested Korean opportunity: Realworld Studio advertises that capability alongside GPS, AR, QR, and NFC features. [Realworld Studio](https://studio.realworld.to/)

The opportunity is a specific customer proposition whose superiority remains unproven: polished neighborhood adventures for local social outings, supported by dependable operations and worthwhile creator and merchant participation.

| Strategic action | Proposed choice |
| --- | --- |
| Eliminate | Mandatory purchases for essential clues; hidden costs; unsupported claims of novelty |
| Reduce | Long screen-reading sessions, complicated onboarding, and staff-dependent tasks |
| Raise | Story quality, meaningful deduction, route reliability, and transparent expectations |
| Create | A repeatable local series with shared-evidence gameplay and measurable merchant value |

This framework describes intended value creation. It does not claim competitors lack these attributes.

Before expanding investment, play comparable Korean and international experiences and test available creator workflows. Record where participants prefer this proposal, where they prefer competitors, and whether the difference changes willingness to pay.

## 8. Differentiation and Defensibility

Gilloe’s initial advantage must be visible within one session: a better story, more satisfying teamwork, or a more dependable outing.

Five commitments could support that advantage:

1. **Place-specific puzzles.** Use observable details that contribute to reasoning rather than arbitrary check-ins.
2. **Social participation.** Give each person something useful to contribute without requiring separation into unsafe or inconvenient routes.
3. **Editorial standards.** Publish fewer strong adventures and maintain them consistently.
4. **Natural merchant participation.** Let a venue support the fiction while keeping purchases optional.
5. **Local continuity.** Give satisfied players another appealing adventure nearby.

Potential longer-term defensibility comes from a maintained catalog, trusted creators, merchant relationships, player referrals, and knowledge of which experiences work in the field. GPS, QR codes, and access to AI are widely available inputs.

The proposed growth loop is:

**More satisfied players → more creator earnings → more good games → more useful visits → stronger merchant participation → better experiences → more satisfied players.**

That loop is a hypothesis. In a location-based product, supply in one city may do little for players in another. Local density and quality matter more than a large global count of inactive listings.

## 9. Game Examples

All examples in this section are fictional concepts. They do not describe real crimes, confirmed partners, surveyed routes, or launched products.

### Pilot: The Seongsu Murder

| Detail | Proposed specification |
| --- | --- |
| Genre | Fictional detective mystery |
| Duration | 60–90 minutes |
| Distance | Approximately 1.5–2 km |
| Team | 2–4 players |
| Stops | 5–7 |
| Initial language | Korean |
| Initial price experiment | ₩4,900–₩7,900 per team |
| Merchant requirement | None for the first route; optional integration later |

A fictional victim’s last movements become the subject of an investigation. The team receives a briefing, checks a visible detail at the first stop, and reconstructs a timeline. Later evidence contradicts a suspect’s account. At the final stop, the team must identify the culprit and explain which evidence supports that conclusion.

In a later partner version, a café marker unlocks a fictional receipt: two drinks at 19:42. The clue suggests the victim met someone. It should combine with other evidence rather than instantly identify the culprit. The receipt is a story prop; it is not a real customer record.

If the café is closed, a fallback delivers the same essential information. Buying a drink can unlock a cosmetic badge or a clearly disclosed optional perk, but the mystery remains fully solvable without it.

### Further concepts

| Concept | Location idea | Player appeal | Design consideration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The Missing Painter | Bukchon | Art and historical mystery | Separate invented plot from verified history; protect residential quiet |
| The Missing Musician | Hongdae/Yeonnam | A music-centered disappearance | Use original or licensed audio |
| 11:47 | Hongdae | Supernatural suspense | Create atmosphere without unsafe late-night routing |
| Why We Parted | Seongsu | A couple’s branching relationship story | Allow private choices without pressuring personal disclosure |
| The Gangnam Conspiracy | Gangnam | Cooperative investigation with different roles | Begin with an existing friend group before stranger matching |
| Haeundae Treasure Trail | Busan | Daytime family exploration | Adjust for crowding, weather, and children’s pace |
| The Distillery District Disappearance | Toronto, as a future example | Local mystery in an international setting | Require local authorship and route validation |

Fantasy, cultural discovery, educational missions, and corporate challenges could follow. The pilot should establish one strong format before broadening genres.

## 10. AI Creator Tools

The Gilloe creator experience could begin with a brief:

> Create a 90-minute mystery for couples in Seongsu, using six approved locations and two participating cafés. Make the puzzles moderately challenging and the essential clues available without purchases.

AI could propose characters, a timeline, clue dependencies, dialogue, hints, alternative endings, and listing copy. It could help identify contradictions or produce translation drafts.

The human creator remains responsible for selecting locations, checking permissions and facts, ensuring puzzle solvability, and walking the route. AI cannot confirm that an entrance is open, a sign still exists, or a venue has agreed to participate.

### Proposed workflow

**Brief → approved location set → story draft → puzzle review → field test → editorial approval → publication → maintenance.**

Useful quality checks include whether the conclusion follows from available evidence, whether hints escalate fairly, and whether a broken stop can be bypassed without breaking the story. Public historical claims need sources. Fictional dialogue should never imply that a real business or resident committed wrongdoing.

Because a Korean competitor already advertises AI-assisted creation, the meaningful test is whether these tools reduce the time and cost of producing an approved, enjoyable route. [Realworld Studio](https://studio.realworld.to/)

An invitation-only trial with a few creators should measure time to first playable draft, editorial correction time, field-test failures, and player response. Counting generated stories would reward volume rather than usable content.

## 11. Creator Marketplace

The Gilloe marketplace could eventually allow approved creators to publish free or paid games. Listings should include a creator profile, route metadata, real player reviews, maintenance status, and a clear description of the purchase.

Creators would manage their stories and location updates. The platform would provide distribution, payments, support rules, moderation, and performance reporting. Contracts would need to define content rights, payout calculations, refunds, maintenance responsibilities, and any use of creator work for AI systems.

Supply should expand gradually:

1. Founder-produced adventures establish the standard.
2. A small invited creator cohort tests the workflow.
3. Successful creators publish second games with less assistance.
4. Wider access follows a workable review and maintenance process.

Discovery should consider quality, availability, and relevance, not only sales volume. New creators need a fair chance to be discovered, while players need protection from abandoned routes and misleading listings.

The earlier concept explored creator shares around 60–70% and a platform share around 20–30%, with other costs accounted for separately. These are negotiation hypotheses. A final policy must define the revenue base and ensure all allocations add up correctly.

## 12. Local Business Integration

Businesses can participate as story locations, optional rest stops, reward partners, or sponsors. Suitable partners might include cafés, independent shops, restaurants, photo booths, and cultural venues.

The first merchant experiment should involve one or two venues after player demand appears. Offer a limited free trial, agree on permitted placement and hours, and give staff a simple explanation. The routine experience should not require staff to become performers or troubleshoot the game.

### Protect the experience

- Include essential clues in the purchased game.
- Make optional spending visible before checkout.
- Provide a closure fallback and an easy way for the venue to pause participation.
- Keep sponsored placements transparent.
- Avoid blocking entrances, occupying scarce seating, or disrupting regular customers.

### Measure business value

Track verified participating-team visits, optional redemptions, attributable purchases where measurement is agreed, and staff burden. A redeemed coupon is evidence of redemption; it does not establish that the sale would not otherwise have occurred.

Where practical, compare similar operating periods with and without the activity. The important commercial question is whether the venue values the incremental contribution enough to renew at a stated price.

Longer-term sponsorship could be shared between the creator and platform under a separate agreement. Consumer ticket sales and merchant sponsorship should remain separate revenue lines so the same money is not counted twice.

## 13. Multiplayer and Social Mechanics

Asymmetric information gives cooperation a purpose. One player might receive a photograph, another an audio transcript, another a coded note, and another a timeline. The solution requires combining those pieces.

For the first test, distribute roles within a pre-existing group that stays together. Role cards or separate evidence views can test the mechanic before synchronized multi-device systems exist.

Later scheduled events could support strangers, language exchanges, university groups, or team-building. These require clear hosting, group-size rules, public meeting points, no-show handling, reporting, and a graceful exit. A game must still conclude if someone leaves or a phone fails.

Replay should usually mean buying another story. Once a mystery is solved, repeated play of the same route may have limited appeal. A rotating series, varied genres, and creator followings are more credible retention mechanisms than assuming endless replay of one plot.

## 14. Monetization and Unit Economics

| Revenue stream | Payer | Sequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First-party game tickets | Players | First paid validation |
| Marketplace commission | Deducted from creator game sales | After creator validation |
| Merchant sponsorship | Participating businesses | After a measurable free pilot |
| Sponsored adventures | Brands or venue operators | Once delivery is repeatable |
| Corporate events | Employers | Separate service pilot |
| Tourism and cultural projects | Institutions | Selective contracts |
| Premium creator tools | Professional creators | After demonstrated workflow value |

The initial ₩4,900–₩7,900 **per-team** range is a low-friction experiment. It should not be treated as a sustainable permanent price. The broader discussion considered roughly ₩4,900–₩15,000 for games; later tests must specify the entitlement and format rather than mixing per-person and per-team prices.

### Illustrative creator-game calculation

Assume **₩7,000 in distributable receipts** remains from a sale after clearly specified transaction-level deductions. This is an arithmetic example, not a tax or payment-fee estimate.

| Allocation | Assumed share | Amount |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Creator | 70% | ₩4,900 |
| Platform | 30% | ₩2,100 |
| Total | 100% | ₩7,000 |

If platform support and hosting attributable to that sale cost ₩600, then ₩1,500 remains before acquisition, fixed overhead, and other operating expenses. Acquisition spending above that amount would require repeat purchases or another contribution source to break even.

For founder-authored games, retain a realistic content-production cost even when no external royalty is paid. Founder labor is not free simply because it does not immediately leave the bank account.

### Potential Profit at Different Marketplace Sizes

The following monthly scenarios show what Gilloe could earn under explicit assumptions. They are **illustrative operating models, not forecasts or market-size estimates**. Bigger user numbers do not create profit automatically: conversion, price, creator payouts, merchant renewal, and acquisition costs determine the result.

Here, a **host** means a creator maintaining published games, not a paid on-site facilitator. Hosted live events would need separate staffing charges. A **user** means a monthly active individual, including people who browse without playing. The model assumes 20% join a paid game, three individuals form a team, and each paying individual plays once that month. Thus 3,000 active users produce 600 paid participants and 200 team purchases.

#### Scale and price assumptions

| Monthly input | Early neighborhood business | Established city business | Larger multi-city business |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Monthly active users | 3,000 | 30,000 | 300,000 |
| Paid participants, at 20% | 600 | 6,000 | 60,000 |
| Paying teams, at 3 people per team | 200 | 2,000 | 20,000 |
| Average team ticket price | ₩14,900 | ₩19,900 | ₩24,900 |
| Effective ticket cost per participant | About ₩4,967 | About ₩6,633 | ₩8,300 |
| Active hosts | 5 | 40 | 250 |
| Maintained games | 10 | 80 | 500 |
| Average monthly purchases per game | 20 | 25 | 40 |
| Paying participating stores | 10 | 80 | 500 |
| Monthly fee per store, excluding applicable tax | ₩50,000 | ₩100,000 | ₩150,000 |

These later commercial prices are deliberately higher than the introductory ₩4,900–₩7,900 team pilot. Whether customers accept them must be tested. Store counts mean **actually paying stores**, not free partners or prospective leads. Hosts are assumed to pay no tool subscription in this model.

#### Revenue-sharing assumptions

Set aside 10% of gross ticket collections as a simplified allowance for payment costs, refunds, and indirect-tax effects. This is a scenario input, not a quoted fee or tax rate; actual tax-inclusive receipts and refunds need their own calculation. Split the remaining 90% as **70% to hosts and 30% to Gilloe**.

For store fees, assume 20% is allocated to the relevant host and 80% to Gilloe. Store fees below are net of applicable indirect tax; collection and merchant-service expenses are covered within the operating allowances. No corporate-event, subscription, or institutional revenue is included.

**Gilloe retained revenue = team purchases × ticket price × 90% × 30% + paying stores × monthly fee × 80%.**

#### Monthly operating model

All figures below are **millions of Korean won**, rounded to three decimals. Profit is calculated before corporate income tax, financing, and one-time startup investment. Labor and routine operating costs are included through explicit allowances.

| Monthly amount | Early neighborhood | Established city | Larger multi-city |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Gross consumer ticket collections | 2.980 | 39.800 | 498.000 |
| Ticket deductions allowance, 10% | 0.298 | 3.980 | 49.800 |
| Host ticket payout | 1.877 | 25.074 | 313.740 |
| **Gilloe ticket share** | **0.805** | **10.746** | **134.460** |
| Store fees collected | 0.500 | 8.000 | 75.000 |
| Host share of store fees | 0.100 | 1.600 | 15.000 |
| **Gilloe store-fee share** | **0.400** | **6.400** | **60.000** |
| **Total Gilloe retained revenue** | **1.205** | **17.146** | **194.460** |
| Acquisition allowance: ₩2,000 per paid team | 0.400 | 4.000 | 40.000 |
| Variable support/tools: ₩500 per paid team | 0.100 | 1.000 | 10.000 |
| Hosting, database, storage, and monitoring allowance | 0.100 | 0.400 | 2.000 |
| AI creation allowance | 0.050 | 0.300 | 2.000 |
| Labor, merchant sales, editorial work, and other overhead | 1.500 | 10.000 | 60.000 |
| **Total modeled operating costs** | **2.150** | **15.700** | **114.000** |
| **Modeled monthly operating profit / loss** | **−0.945** | **1.446** | **80.460** |
| **Annualized at the same monthly level** | **−11.345** | **17.352** | **965.520** |

The annualized row simply multiplies by 12. It assumes no seasonality, growth, or ramp-up and is not a first-year prediction. The ₩1.5 million early labor/overhead allowance represents limited founder time, not a fully staffed company. Expand it if actual staffing or professional-service requirements are greater. International expansion could substantially increase labor, localization, payments, and support costs beyond this model.

The lesson is that a small marketplace may lose money even with low hosting costs. The established-city scenario is only modestly profitable. The larger scenario has attractive arithmetic, but obtaining 300,000 active users, 250 productive hosts, and 500 renewing stores is a substantial unvalidated business challenge.

#### Sensitivity and break-even

In the established-city case, a ₩19,900 team purchase contributes **₩2,873** after the ticket deduction allowance, host payout, ₩2,000 acquisition allowance, and ₩500 variable service cost. Fixed monthly costs are ₩10.7 million, and Gilloe retains ₩6.4 million of store fees.

With those store fees held constant, break-even is approximately **1,497 paid teams per month**, or **22,455 active individuals** at the assumed conversion and team size. Without store revenue, it rises to approximately **3,725 teams**, or **55,875 active individuals**. These calculations hold costs and prices fixed; additional demand may require more staffing.

| Base case | ₩1.446 million |

| Acquisition allowance rises from ₩2,000 to ₩5,000 per paid team | −₩4.554 million |

Holding store fees constant when player traffic falls is optimistic: weak traffic could also cause merchant cancellations. Conversely, organic repeat purchases could reduce the blended acquisition expense. Measure these effects rather than assuming they cancel out.

### Server Costs for Korea and International Use

#### What determines the bill

Registered users alone do not determine server requirements. The main variables are active sessions, peak concurrency, requests per session, database work, downloaded media, and whether AI runs during play. A story game with occasional clue submissions can be inexpensive; live voice, frequent location updates, video, and continuous AI dialogue change the workload substantially.

For an initial text-and-image product, assume **200 dynamic requests and 20 MB of media per monthly active user**, including browsing and play. This is a sizing assumption, not a measured benchmark.

| Monthly active users | Dynamic requests | Media delivered before caching savings |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 3,000 | 600,000 | About 60 GB |
| 30,000 | 6 million | About 600 GB |
| 300,000 | 60 million | About 6 TB |

This does not include frequent multiplayer polling. For example, 1,000 connected devices polling every five seconds generate about 200 requests per second before other activity. Prefer event-driven updates for live play, and load-test expected weekend peaks before choosing capacity.

#### Current price anchors

AWS Lightsail lists a Linux server with 4 GB memory and public IPv4 at **US$24/month**, an encrypted 2 GB managed database at **US$30/month**, its high-availability version at **US$60/month**, and snapshots at **US$0.05 per GB-month**. These are reference components, not a complete production quote. Transfer allowances, regional availability, taxes, and extra services must be checked for the selected deployment. [AWS Lightsail pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/lightsail/pricing/)

Cloudflare Workers Paid starts at **US$5/month** and includes 10 million requests and 30 million CPU milliseconds. Additional requests cost US$0.30 per million, and additional CPU time costs US$0.02 per million CPU milliseconds. At 60 million dynamic requests and an assumed 10 ms CPU time each, the compute estimate is **US$31.40/month**. That excludes databases, storage, other services, and AI inference. [Workers pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/)

Cloudflare R2 standard storage is listed at **US$0.015 per GB-month**, with separately priced operations and no Internet egress charge. For example, 100 GB costs US$1.50 for storage before free allowances and operation charges. This does not make the entire application free to operate. [R2 pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)

It is a small starting configuration, not a guarantee of availability or capacity. Two application instances, a load balancer, and a redundant database increase reliability and cost.

AI should normally generate reviewed content before publication. Prewritten stories and hints avoid paying for repeated generation during every player session. The profit model includes separate AI budgets; actual usage depends on the selected model, tokens, media generation, and creator activity.

### How Gilloe Can Run in Different Server Regions

**A cloud region is a physical deployment area; a CDN is a distributed cache.** A content delivery network can place copies of images and published game files near players, while the main application and database remain in Seoul. Global content delivery does not mean account data or live game state has been replicated into every country.

AWS documents Lightsail regions including Seoul, Tokyo, Singapore, Canada Central, Northern Virginia, and Frankfurt. Region selection is available when creating resources; multiple availability zones within a region provide a different kind of redundancy from running in multiple countries. [AWS regions and availability zones](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/lightsail/latest/userguide/understanding-regions-and-availability-zones-in-amazon-lightsail.html)

#### Stage 1: Korea-first

**Players in Korea → nearby content cache → Gilloe application and operational database in Seoul.**

Keep the architecture simple. An overseas visitor playing a Korean route can use the Seoul backend as well; the player’s nationality does not determine server placement. Inspect backup, analytics, and vendor configurations separately if a particular data-location commitment is required.

#### Stage 2: International access without duplicating everything

**Players worldwide → global content cache → one primary application/database region.**

This may be adequate for asynchronous mysteries with occasional answer submission. Measure actual response times from target cities. A foreign audience does not automatically require another backend, especially while only a small number of games exist abroad.

An edge-compute alternative such as Cloudflare Workers executes application functions on a distributed network, but database placement remains a separate design choice. Global execution alone does not solve distant database access or establish a Korea-only data boundary. [How Workers works](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/reference/how-workers-works/)

#### Stage 3: Regional game operations

| Market | Example operational region | Data owned there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Korea | Seoul | Korean game sessions and regional operational records |
| North America | Canada Central or a suitable US region | North American game sessions and regional records |
| Europe | Frankfurt or another appropriate European region | European game sessions and regional records |

Deploy the same application release to each selected region. Use a shared directory to identify where a game session belongs, and route the whole team to that region. Publish reviewed game content to global caches. Aggregate reporting can flow back asynchronously without copying every precise location event worldwide.

Latency-aware routing can direct eligible requests to a lower-latency regional endpoint; AWS Route 53 documents this routing option. Existing sessions should remain attached to their owning region, rather than moving whenever a connection changes. [Route 53 latency routing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/routing-policy-latency.html)

Give each live session one authoritative writer. A team’s clue progress, prize redemption, and purchase entitlement should not be updated independently in multiple regions without explicit conflict handling. Read-only replicas can help browsing, but they may lag and should not be treated as the authority for a payment or reward.

Choose deliberately whether accounts and payments use one central system or regional records with a shared identity layer. Cross-region replicas, backups, and analytics transfers add costs and need security and data-handling review. An ordinary Lightsail database is not automatically a global synchronized database; that requires additional application design or a service built for it.

Finally, distinguish **regional resilience** from **regional expansion**. A backup copy is not an immediately usable failover service. Test restoration and define acceptable data loss and recovery time. Add regions when measured latency, local operations, or data requirements justify the complexity—not merely because an international marketing page exists.

## 15. MVP Validation Plan

### A six-to-eight-week experiment

| Period | Work | Evidence sought |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Week 1 | Interview likely users, compare alternatives, inspect a route | A specific purchase occasion and feasible setting |
| Week 2 | Write one game and complete route walk-throughs | A coherent experience with workable clues |
| Weeks 3–4 | Test with 10–20 people for free; revise | Completion, enjoyment, and understandable puzzles |
| Weeks 5–6 | Offer a clearly priced game to unrelated customers | Approximately 20 actual paying teams |
| Weeks 7–8 | Test one or two venues; offer a second story or booking | Merchant fit and behavioral repeat-interest evidence |

Recruit enough separate teams to observe different group dynamics. Ten to twenty individuals are not the same as ten to twenty independent sessions.

### Minimal delivery

A mobile-friendly page, map directions, clues, answer entry, hints, and progress are sufficient. Use existing payment and communication services as appropriate. Native apps, elaborate GPS gating, AR, creator dashboards, merchant dashboards, and recommendation systems can wait.

The first route can use public-facing observations without merchant dependencies. Obtain permission before placing markers or involving private premises. Test at the actual time of day intended for customers.

ClueKeeper’s published guidance also emphasizes testing with people who do not already know the answers, at the real locations. That is a useful category practice. [ClueKeeper testing guidelines](https://cluekeeper.com/self-guided-hunts)

## 16. Metrics

Use **teams** as the main unit for sales and game sessions, and **individuals** for personal survey responses. Keep free, paid, and hosted cohorts separate.

| Metric | Definition | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paid conversion | Paying teams divided by qualified prospective teams shown the offer | Demand at a disclosed price |
| Activation | Teams beginning the first clue divided by teams with access | Onboarding and attendance |
| Completion | Teams reaching the ending divided by teams that started | Overall experience reliability |
| Rescue rate | Started teams needing unplanned human help divided by all started teams | Hidden operating burden |
| Duration | Median and spread of start-to-finish time | Whether the promised outing is accurate |
| Enjoyment | Individual post-game rating, with response count | Perceived quality |
| Repeat purchase | Eligible paid teams booking another available game within 30 days | Behavioral retention |
| Referral conversion | New paying teams attributable to a participant referral | Word-of-mouth effectiveness |
| Contribution per team | Receipts less defined variable costs | Financial viability |
| Merchant continuation | Trial venues choosing a further defined trial or paid offer | Partnership value |
| Creator efficiency | Hours and review effort per approved route | Whether tools improve production |

At each stop, note wrong-answer patterns, hint usage, navigation confusion, and abandonment. Distinguish an intentionally challenging puzzle from a broken instruction.

The first operating north-star metric can be **paid teams completing an adventure without unplanned staff rescue**. Pair it with enjoyment and contribution; completion alone can conceal a dull or subsidized experience.

## 17. Go, Pivot, and No-Go Criteria

These are proposed decision rules for a small pilot, not industry benchmarks. Agree on them before reviewing outcomes, and record raw counts alongside percentages.

| Dimension | Proposed positive signal | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand | 20 unrelated paying teams, primarily at ₩5,000 or more per team | Enough evidence for another focused experiment |
| Completion | At least 80% of paid starts finish | For 20 starts, at least 16 completions |
| Independence | At least 80% finish without unplanned staff rescue | The route can plausibly become self-guided |
| Enjoyment | At least 70% of respondents rate enjoyment 4/5 or 5/5 | Report nonresponse and individual counts |
| Repeat behavior | At least 4 of 20 eligible paid teams book a second available game within 30 days | Requires an actual offer and adequate follow-up time |
| Economics | Positive contribution before acquisition and a credible route to cover labor and acquisition | Low ticket prices may need revision |
| Merchant fit | At least one trial venue wants to continue on explicit terms | Paid sponsorship still needs its own validation |

**GO:** If the core game and demand signals are encouraging, fund a second adventure and a small creator trial. Twenty sales do not justify an unrestricted marketplace build.

**PIVOT:** If enjoyment is strong but consumer payment is weak, test a different price, purchase unit, audience, or sponsored format. Corporate and tourism buyers need their own paid evidence; they are not automatic fallback customers.

**NO-GO or pause:** If completion and enjoyment remain weak after two meaningful redesigns, or delivery cannot be made safe and affordable, stop additional platform investment. Diagnose whether the failed assumption concerns the particular route, story, audience, or broader model.

If demand is promising but the sample is incomplete, extend the bounded experiment with a defined deadline. A missed recruitment target alone does not prove the game is poor.

## 18. Risks and Operating Principles

| Risk | Practical response |
| --- | --- |
| Boring or incoherent content | Blind playtests, editorial review, and measured revisions |
| Marketplace cold start | Produce the first routes; invite creators gradually |
| Strong competitors | Compare actual experiences and creation workflows; avoid novelty-based positioning |
| Weather and seasonal discomfort | Publish operating limits, rescheduling rules, and shorter alternatives |
| Closures or changed landmarks | Assign maintenance ownership and provide equivalent fallback clues |
| Unsafe movement or trespass | Walk-test routes, avoid hazardous tasks, and use authorized spaces |
| Accessibility mismatch | Describe distances, steps, surfaces, rest points, and alternatives accurately |
| Privacy and stranger interaction | Minimize location collection; avoid public live tracking; provide reporting and exit options |
| Merchant disruption | Limit staff tasks and group congestion; allow immediate pause |
| AI errors or copied content | Human review, rights checks, and verified historical sources |
| Weak margins | Track creator, support, maintenance, and acquisition costs separately |
| Sponsored content undermining trust | Label sponsorship; keep the essential game complete without extra purchases |
| Reward abuse | Simple redemption limits and clear eligibility rules |

Before commercial launch, obtain the appropriate local review of privacy and location-data handling, consumer terms, payments, promotion mechanics, content rights, and insurance needs. The required approach depends on the actual service design; this document does not assert a particular legal classification.

The operating principle is straightforward: the route is part of the product. Its safety, access, upkeep, and treatment of neighbors deserve the same attention as the story.

## 23. Long-Term Vision

Gilloe’s long-term vision is a way to choose an adventure as easily as choosing a film. A player opens the service, finds a nearby story that fits the group’s time and interests, and steps outside.

Local creators could build recognizable series and earn from original work. Businesses could participate because their space strengthens an experience and the resulting visits are worthwhile. Cultural organizations could use the same medium for interpretation and participation.

International growth would depend on local authorship and operating care. A creator who knows Toronto or Tokyo brings knowledge that cannot be replaced by remotely generated location suggestions.

The vision is ambitious, but the next question is small and observable: **Will unfamiliar customers pay for one well-designed adventure, finish it happily, and choose another?**

That answer should determine how much of the platform to build next.

## Part II — Safety, Security, and Risk Management
### Building a Safer Location-Based Real-World Adventure Platform

**Published:** September 10, 2026  
**Project stage:** Concept validation and pilot planning  
**Audience:** Players, creators, participating businesses, partners, and project stakeholders

> A real-world game must treat the real world as part of the product.

Gilloe turns neighborhoods into playable worlds. Players walk through real places, follow stories, discover clues, and solve challenges together. That creates an experience that can feel more memorable than a game played entirely on a screen. It also creates responsibilities that ordinary digital games do not have.

The most serious risks are physical injury, misuse of location information, unsafe creator-made routes, harmful interactions between strangers, and failures that leave players stranded during an experience. These risks are manageable, but safety and privacy must be built into Gilloe from the first pilot.

This document describes proposed product and operating principles. It is not legal, insurance, or cybersecurity advice. Gilloe’s exact obligations will depend on its final design, data practices, audience, and markets.

### TL;DR

- Gilloe’s highest-priority risks are physical player safety, location privacy, stranger interactions, and unsafe creator content.
- The first pilot should run during daylight on a field-tested public route for pre-existing groups of 2–4 adults.
- Gilloe should check location only while a game is active and avoid storing a detailed history of each player’s movements.
- Essential clues should never require a purchase, a staff interaction, or access to a single location with no fallback.
- Creator games should be reviewed and physically tested before publication.
- Stranger matching, minors, valuable prizes, continuous tracking, and open self-publishing should come later, after dedicated safeguards exist.
- Payments should use a trusted payment provider so Gilloe does not store card numbers.
- Gilloe needs a way to pause a route immediately when a location becomes unsafe or unavailable.

### 1. Risk Overview

| Risk | Example | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical safety | A player crosses a road while reading a clue or enters an unsafe area | Critical |
| Location privacy | A breach reveals where users played and when | Critical |
| Stranger interactions | Harassment, stalking, inappropriate behavior, or unsafe meetings | Critical |
| Creator content | A creator sends players onto private property or through a dangerous route | High |
| Merchant conflict | Players disturb customers, staff misunderstand the activity, or a venue closes | High |
| Account security | Someone takes over a creator account, changes games, or redirects payouts | High |
| QR manipulation | Someone covers a Gilloe marker with a malicious QR code | High |
| Payment and reward fraud | Stolen cards, repeated coupon redemption, fake completion, or refund abuse | Medium–high |
| Service failure | A route stops working while players are outside | Medium–high |
| AI-generated errors | False historical claims, impossible clues, offensive content, or nonexistent locations | Medium–high |
| Weak economics | Safety, support, moderation, and maintenance cost more than the game earns | High |

One serious injury or location-data incident could damage player trust and merchant relationships before Gilloe has time to establish itself. Safety and security work therefore protect both users and the business.

### 2. Physical Player Safety

Gilloe should never require players to read, type, photograph, or solve a puzzle while crossing a road or moving through a crowded area. Clues should activate at safe stopping points where a team can gather without blocking other people.

Every route should receive an on-site review for:

- Road crossings, vehicle entrances, bicycle paths, and construction
- Stairs, slopes, uneven surfaces, low lighting, and seasonal hazards
- Private property, restricted areas, and residential disruption
- Weather exposure, available shelter, and safe operating hours
- Public transportation and a clear way to leave the route
- Accessibility information, rest points, and alternative paths
- Crowd capacity at each stop
- Mobile reception and battery requirements

#### Route operating rules

A route should have defined operating hours rather than being described as available at any time. A route that is appropriate at 2:00 p.m. may be unsafe or disruptive at 11:00 p.m.

Gilloe should pause a route during severe weather, construction, public emergencies, unusual crowding, or changes that make the published path unsafe. Players need an obvious **Stop Game** option that provides directions back to a safe transportation point.

Safety instructions should be short and shown when relevant. Constant warnings may cause users to ignore the message that matters. For example, the application can require players to confirm that they have stopped walking before displaying a puzzle near a road.

#### Liability and insurance

Terms and waivers can explain ordinary risks, but they do not replace safe design, reasonable maintenance, or appropriate insurance. Before accepting commercial bookings, Gilloe should discuss public-liability coverage, participant injury, property damage, creator responsibility, and event coverage with qualified Korean legal and insurance professionals.

### 3. Location Privacy

Location history can reveal where a person lives, works, studies, socializes, or travels. A precise record of movement may be far more sensitive than a completed-game badge.

Gilloe should follow a minimum-data approach:

- Request location only while a location-dependent game is active.
- Avoid background location permission unless a future feature can demonstrate a clear need.
- Verify that a device is near a checkpoint without storing the complete route taken between checkpoints.
- Store a checkpoint completion record instead of continuous latitude and longitude whenever possible.
- Do not display one player’s precise live location to strangers.
- Keep precise coordinates out of ordinary analytics, customer-support screens, and application logs.
- Delete temporary location events according to a short, documented retention schedule.
- Let users withdraw consent and request deletion through an understandable process.
- Explain which service providers receive data and why.

Korea has specific rules governing location information. The official law addresses consent, minimum necessary collection, protection measures, user rights, and reporting requirements for certain location-related businesses. Gilloe should determine its current legal classification before launch because the requirements depend on how it obtains and uses location information. [Act on the Protection and Use of Location Information](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/lsInfoP.do?chrClsCd=010202&lsiSeq=236317&urlMode=engLsInfoR&viewCls=engLsInfoR)

Korea’s Personal Information Protection Act also requires clear handling of personal information and recognizable consent requests where consent is the relevant basis. [Personal Information Protection Act](https://www.law.go.kr/lsInfoP.do?lsiSeq=270351&urlMode=engLsInfoR&viewCls=engLsInfoR)

### 4. Multiplayer and Stranger Safety

Playing with existing friends is materially different from meeting unknown participants. Stranger matching can introduce harassment, stalking, discrimination, pressure to share contact details, inappropriate photography, and unsafe behavior after an event.

Gilloe should begin with private groups whose members already know one another. If scheduled stranger games are introduced later, they should include:

- Adults-only participation during the initial release
- Public, staffed, or highly visible meeting points
- Group formats rather than default one-to-one matching
- Blocking, reporting, and participant-removal procedures
- No public display of phone numbers, home locations, or precise live positions
- Clear behavior standards and consequences
- A host or reachable moderator during the event
- A safe way for any participant to leave
- A fallback that lets the remaining team finish if someone leaves or fails to arrive

Gilloe should not describe a participant-matching feature as safe merely because identity information has been collected. Identity checks can discourage some abuse, but they cannot predict behavior or replace moderation and careful event design.

### 5. Creator Content Risks

Open creation can expand Gilloe’s catalog, but unrestricted publication would allow unsafe routes, misleading claims, offensive material, fake merchant partnerships, and intellectual-property violations.

New creator games should pass several gates:

1. Automated checks for missing route information and prohibited content
2. Human editorial and safety review
3. Permission confirmation for participating businesses and private locations
4. A complete field test at the intended time of day
5. A blind test by people who do not know the answers
6. Final approval with a named maintenance owner

Every game should have a report button and an immediate unpublishing mechanism. Safety reports should outrank ordinary quality complaints.

Creators should confirm that they own or are authorized to use text, images, music, characters, and other intellectual property. A fictional crime must not make a real person or business appear connected to criminal or harmful conduct.

Published historical claims should be checked against reliable sources. Sensitive cultural locations require additional judgment even when public access is technically permitted.

### 6. Participating Business Risks

A game can send multiple groups into a business during busy periods. Players may block an entrance, occupy seats without purchasing, repeatedly ask staff for help, or search areas that are not open to the public.

Each participating business should have a written operating brief covering:

- Approved location and player activity
- Maximum group size and expected visit frequency
- Permitted hours and blackout periods
- Whether photography is allowed
- What staff are expected to do
- What players may receive or redeem
- Contact details for problems
- A one-step method to pause participation

Staff should not need to remember complicated dialogue or manage the main clue. The essential experience needs a digital fallback if a business is closed, crowded, or temporarily unavailable.

Required purchases can make a paid game feel deceptive and can create consumer disputes. Essential clues should be included in the game price. Optional purchases and rewards should be explained before checkout.

### 7. Application and Account Security

Gilloe will hold account information, game progress, creator content, merchant data, and possibly creator earnings. Different users must only access information and actions appropriate to their role.

Minimum controls should include:

- Encrypted connections for all application and administrative traffic
- Encryption for sensitive stored data
- Secure authentication and optional multi-factor authentication for creators, merchants, and administrators
- Separate access permissions for players, creators, merchants, support staff, and administrators
- Additional verification before changing payout or account-recovery details
- Short-lived sessions and secure account recovery
- Rate limits against automated abuse and credential attacks
- No passwords, private keys, or unrestricted service credentials inside the mobile application
- Security updates for application dependencies
- Audit records for publishing, payout, permission, and administrative changes
- Review of third-party analytics, map, AI, messaging, and advertising components

The OWASP Mobile Application Security Verification Standard provides a practical baseline covering storage, cryptography, authentication, network communication, platform interaction, application code, resilience, and privacy. [OWASP MASVS](https://mas.owasp.org/MASVS/)

OWASP also recommends minimizing access to sensitive resources and limiting third-party data sharing to what the service actually requires. [OWASP privacy control](https://mas.owasp.org/MASVS/controls/MASVS-PRIVACY-1/)

### 8. QR Codes, Clues, and Reward Fraud

Physical QR codes can be covered or replaced with codes leading to malicious websites. Gilloe markers should use recognizable branding and tamper-aware placement. The application should recognize Gilloe links and warn users before opening an unrelated external address.

For stronger protection, a QR code can contain a short-lived or signed value that the server verifies. This does not stop physical replacement, but it prevents an attacker from inventing valid Gilloe completion codes.

Reward systems can attract fake accounts, GPS spoofing, repeated redemptions, screenshots of codes, and coordinated abuse. Controls can include:

- One redemption per eligible account or team
- Short redemption windows
- Server-side completion verification
- Single-use reward tokens
- Merchant confirmation that does not expose customer information
- Limits on unusually valuable rewards
- Manual review for suspicious patterns

Anti-fraud measures should remain proportional. Collecting identity documents for a low-value digital badge would create more privacy risk than the reward justifies.

### 9. Payments and Consumer Protection

Gilloe should use a reputable payment provider and avoid storing card numbers. The platform still needs controls around refunds, creator payouts, stolen accounts, disputed transactions, and merchant billing.

Before payment, players should see:

- Whether the price is per person or per team
- The complete duration and walking distance
- Operating hours and location restrictions
- Any optional or required additional cost
- Weather and cancellation rules
- Accessibility limitations
- Refund treatment when a route or server fails

Korea’s electronic-commerce framework covers information duties, prohibited conduct, and consumer cancellation rights for online transactions. The exact treatment of a digital experience can depend on how and when it is supplied, so Gilloe’s checkout and refund terms should receive local review. [Korea Fair Trade Commission: E-commerce Policy](https://www.ftc.go.kr/eng/contents.do?key=560)

Creator and merchant payouts create additional risks. Gilloe should verify payout changes, delay suspicious transactions, retain appropriate accounting records, and avoid allowing support staff to change financial destinations without oversight.

### 10. AI Content Risks

AI can help creators draft stories and puzzles, but it can also produce false facts, copied material, offensive stereotypes, inaccessible routes, nonexistent locations, or mysteries that cannot logically be solved.

AI-generated content should remain a draft until a human creator and Gilloe reviewer approve it. The review should confirm:

- Every location exists and is safely accessible
- The ending follows from evidence available to the player
- Hints progress from gentle assistance to direct help
- Real people and businesses are not falsely portrayed
- Historical and cultural claims are supported
- Text, images, and audio can legally be used
- Translations preserve safety instructions and puzzle meaning
- A route can survive the loss of any fragile clue or location

Player conversations, photographs, and precise location records should not be sent to an AI provider merely because AI tools are available. Any such use needs a defined purpose, appropriate notice, data controls, and a less intrusive alternative where practical.

### 11. Server Reliability and Incident Response

A server outage during an outdoor activity is more serious than an unavailable marketing page. Players may be far from the starting point, unsure where to go, or unable to retrieve a purchased experience.

Gilloe should provide:

- Cached safety instructions and a route-exit option
- A fallback for each essential clue
- Health monitoring and alerts
- Regular backups and tested restoration
- A way to disable a route without taking down the entire platform
- A public support channel during operating hours
- A written incident-response process
- A record of who can access production systems and user data

An incident plan should cover physical emergencies, data exposure, payment problems, harmful creator content, malicious QR replacement, unavailable merchants, and severe service interruption.

After a material incident, Gilloe should preserve evidence, limit further harm, identify affected users, and follow applicable notification requirements. The team should rehearse this process before it is needed.

### 12. International and Regional Data Risks

Adding servers in another region can improve response time, but it can also create additional copies of personal information, backups, logs, and administrative access. A global content-delivery network does not by itself determine where account or location data is stored.

Gilloe should document:

- Which region owns a live game session
- Where accounts, payments, support records, and location events are stored
- Which vendors and staff can access each category
- Whether information is copied across national borders
- How long regional backups remain
- How deletion reaches replicas and backups

An international player using a Korean route does not automatically require a separate foreign server. Gilloe can initially keep the operational system in Seoul and distribute nonsensitive images and published game content through a global cache. Additional application and database regions should follow measured demand, legal review, and a clear operating need.

### 13. First-Pilot Safety Design

The safest useful first pilot is intentionally limited:

| Area | Pilot rule |
| --- | --- |
| Players | Pre-existing groups of 2–4 adults |
| Time | Daylight and clearly published operating hours |
| Route | Public, field-tested paths with safe stopping points |
| Location access | Only while the game is active; no continuous route history |
| Multiplayer | Private groups; no stranger matching |
| Purchases | No required store purchase |
| Merchants | None initially, or one explicitly approved low-burden partner |
| Prizes | No high-value rewards |
| Creators | Founder-produced game; no public self-publishing |
| Clues | Digital fallback for every essential stop |
| Support | Reachable contact and immediate route shutdown capability |
| Weather | Defined pause, rescheduling, and refund policy |

This design still tests the essential question: whether people enjoy and pay for a Gilloe adventure. It postpones features that add substantial risk without being necessary for the first learning milestone.

### 14. Pre-Launch Checklist

#### Route and physical safety

- [ ] The complete route has been walked at the intended operating time.
- [ ] Every clue can be solved from a safe stopping position.
- [ ] No task encourages unsafe road crossing or trespass.
- [ ] Construction, weather, darkness, and closure risks have been assessed.
- [ ] Accessibility, distance, stairs, and surfaces are described accurately.
- [ ] Every essential stop has a fallback.
- [ ] Players can stop and return to transportation safely.

#### Privacy and security

- [ ] Location access is limited to the minimum necessary.
- [ ] Continuous GPS trails are not retained by default.
- [ ] Consent and privacy explanations use clear language.
- [ ] Sensitive information is absent from ordinary logs.
- [ ] Account, creator, merchant, and administrator permissions are separated.
- [ ] Payment-card information is handled by the payment provider.
- [ ] Creator and administrator accounts have stronger authentication.
- [ ] Backups and restoration have been tested.
- [ ] An incident-response contact and procedure exist.

#### Content and operations

- [ ] People unfamiliar with the answers have completed a blind field test.
- [ ] The conclusion follows logically from available evidence.
- [ ] Merchant and private-location permissions are documented.
- [ ] Fiction cannot reasonably be mistaken for an accusation against a real person or business.
- [ ] Content rights and factual claims have been reviewed.
- [ ] The team can pause or remove the route immediately.
- [ ] Support and refund procedures are ready.

#### Commercial readiness

- [ ] Price, purchase unit, additional costs, and cancellation rules are visible.
- [ ] Insurance and legal needs have been reviewed for the actual pilot design.
- [ ] Creator and merchant responsibilities are documented where applicable.
- [ ] Safety, moderation, support, and maintenance costs are included in the business model.

### 15. Recommended Sequence

Gilloe should add risk in stages rather than launching every capability together.

1. Test one daytime route with private adult groups.
2. Add payment and a clear refund process.
3. Add one approved merchant with a complete fallback.
4. Test invited creators under manual review.
5. Introduce low-value rewards with fraud controls.
6. Add scheduled private-group events.
7. Pilot moderated stranger groups with dedicated safeguards.
8. Consider minors only with age-appropriate design and verified guardian requirements.
9. Add regional servers when measured demand or data requirements justify them.

This sequence gives Gilloe time to learn from controlled sessions before exposing a large community to untested routes or social mechanics.
